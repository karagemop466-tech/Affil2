The temperature chart that actually matters (and it is not on Amazon)

A meat thermometer is only as useful as the number you are cooking towards. That number does not come from an Amazon listing and it should not come from a blog. In the United States it comes from the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, which publishes a table of safe minimum internal temperatures.

The short answer

Cook to the USDA chart, calibrate the probe in ice water, and let whole cuts rest. A faster thermometer does not change the target.

The minimums, quoted

Poultry, including ground poultry, giblets and stuffing: 165 °F (73.9 °C). Ground meats: 160 °F (71.1 °C). Beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts: 145 °F (62.8 °C) with a rest of at least three minutes. Fish and shellfish: 145 °F (62.8 °C). Leftovers and casseroles: 165 °F (73.9 °C). Eggs: 160 °F (71.1 °C).

Where those numbers come from

The USDA FSIS Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart, linked in full on every thermometer page on this site and recorded as source S22 in the source register. The chart was last updated in April 2025, and it is the only temperature source this site quotes.

What accuracy you actually need

The chart's targets are whole degrees: 145, 160, 165. A thermometer that reads within one degree is comfortably sufficient for those targets. Anything beyond that is about repeatability and preference, not safety.

Calibrate before you trust it

An ice-water slurry sits at 32 °F at sea level, which makes it a free reference point. If a thermometer reads several degrees off in ice water, it will read several degrees off in a chicken. The ThermoMaven listing ships a calibration certificate; the CHEF iQ listing describes USDA-approved doneness presets in its app.

Rest time is part of the number

For whole cuts the USDA figure includes a rest of at least three minutes. Carry-over cooking continues after the food leaves the heat, which is why pulling a roast at the target rather than above it is the usual practice.
