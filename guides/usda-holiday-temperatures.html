USDA doneness temperatures for the holiday bird

A roast is not done when it looks done; it is done when the thickest part hits the number the USDA sets. Those numbers come from one chart, and they are the same whether you are cooking for two or for twelve. A thermometer's whole job is to remove the guesswork from hitting them.

The short answer

Read the USDA minimums first, then pick the thermometer that lets you hit them without standing at the oven. Calibrate before the cook and measure the thickest part, and the number on the display is the one that matters.

The chart is the source of record

The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service publishes a Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart, checked here on 2026-11-20. It sets poultry — including stuffing and giblets — at 165 °F, ground meats at 160 °F, and beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts at 145 °F with a three-minute rest, fish and shellfish at 145 °F, and leftovers and casseroles at 165 °F. Those five numbers are the target, not a suggestion.

Why the tool changes the conversation

An instant-read thermometer answers one question fast: is it at the number right now. The ThermoMaven here is a 1-second instant-read, stated at ±0.5 °F accuracy with an upper rating of 572 °F (P-04). A wireless thermometer answers a different question: what is it doing while you are not standing at the oven. The CHEF iQ here runs three probes, is rated heat-safe to 1000 °F, and reports through an app (P-05).

Match the tool to the cook

For a single bird and quick checks through the cook, an instant-read is the simpler instrument. For a long roast, a smoker, or several proteins at once, a wireless setup lets you walk away and still know the number. The right tool is the one that lets you hit the chart without standing over the oven for hours.

Two habits that make the numbers real

Calibrate before the holiday, not during it — the USDA FSIS guide to food-thermometer calibration is the reference for the ice-water and boiling-point methods (S24). And insert the probe into the thickest part of the meat, avoiding bone, fat and gristle, because a reading from the wrong spot is a reading you cannot act on.
