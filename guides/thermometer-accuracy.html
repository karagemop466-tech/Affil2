How to assess thermometer accuracy claims

Accuracy is the one specification you cannot check by reading harder. A listing can say ±0.5 °F and reference NIST and NSF, and every word of that can be true while telling you less than you think. Here is a calmer way to read it.

What we would do

Read the claim, then look for the certificate. If there is no certificate you can see, treat the number as a claim and verify it yourself with an ice-water test.

01Separate the claim from the evidence

±0.5 °F is a claim about tolerance. NIST and NSF appearing in a specification-met field is a claim about certification. Neither is a test report, and no listing here links one. That does not make them false — it makes them unaudited by us, which is a different statement.

02Know what the certifications cover

NIST is a standards body; a reference to it usually means traceability of calibration rather than a consumer-product approval. NSF covers public health and sanitation standards for food equipment. A listing that says "NIST and NSF certified" without saying what was certified leaves both open. The ThermoMaven listing ships a calibration certificate in the box, which is the most concrete artefact on offer here.

03Look for a calibration path

A thermometer you can verify is worth more than one you have to trust. The USDA FSIS Food Thermometers guide (source S24) describes the ice-water calibration test: immerse the stem at least 2 inches in an ice-water slurry for 30 seconds without touching the glass; a calibrated thermometer reads 32 °F. A unit that ships with a certificate and a clear calibration path is easier to keep honest.

04Check resolution separately from accuracy

Resolution is how many digits the display shows; accuracy is how close the number is to the truth. A 0.1° display with ±0.5 °F accuracy is not a 0.1° instrument. The ThermoMaven HT07 listing states both, which makes the distinction visible.

05Match the tool to the number you need

USDA's minimum internal temperatures are published in whole degrees: 145, 160, 165. If you are cooking to those targets, a thermometer that is within a degree is comfortably sufficient. Precision beyond that is about repeatability and preference, not safety.
