Reheating leftovers is a temperature, not a microwave guess: the 165-degree rule

The week after a holiday is the biggest thermometer-selling week of the year, and the question is never how crisp the leftover gets. It is whether the middle got hot enough. USDA publishes the number; the appliance listing only publishes a range.

By The Kitchen DeskVerified against the official listing on 2026-11-20Who writes thisHow we work

The short answer

Reheat leftovers and casseroles to 165 °F, measured in the centre with an instrument you have checked, and stir or check in more than one place on anything large or irregular. Everything else — rack position, temperature, how long — is a texture decision, and this site will not dress up a guess as a cook time.

01The number, and the one exception

USDA's safe minimum internal temperature chart puts leftovers and casseroles at 165 °F, the same line it puts poultry and reheated stuffing at. The exception worth knowing is ham: a fully cooked ham packaged in a USDA-inspected plant can be reheated to 140 °F, and all others go to 165 °F. Those are safety thresholds, not quality targets, and no appliance listing on this site is quoted as a source for either.

02The two-hour rule is the half people skip

The FSIS guidance flags perishable food that has sat between 40 °F and 140 °F for more than two hours. Reheating to 165 does not undo a long afternoon on a counter, and nothing in a small hot cavity can. If the plate has been out since the toast, the decision was made hours before the appliance was involved.

03What a temperature range on a listing actually tells you

The ranges we read are settings, not instructions: 200–450 °F on the dual-basket Chefman, 200–400 °F in its specification table against a stated 450 °F Hi-Fry option on the 8-quart (that listing contradicts itself and we print both), and 75–500 °F on the Emeril oven-style unit. The low end of a range is where a gentle, slow reheat lives; the top end is where you get colour. That is as far as the paperwork goes — which setting reaches 165 in the middle of your dish, without drying the edge, is exactly the kind of thing we have not tested.

04Why there are no minutes on this page

Because a minute is a function of the dish, its density, its starting temperature and how much of it you stacked, and because nobody here cooked it. What we will point at is the measurement: in a small cavity, check thick portions in more than one place, stir halfway, and let a standing time do its work. That is the USDA guidance for large and irregular foods, and it survives the loss of the fake precision.

05What to look for in an appliance if reheating is your real job

Not presets. Usable single-layer area, because food stacked in a second layer steams instead of crisping; a window, if you would rather look than open the drawer (the dual-basket unit states two viewing windows, the 8-quart states none); and a low end of the range gentle enough that the outside does not finish before the middle does.
