What a 1700 W appliance means for your kitchen circuit

Air fryers convert electricity into heat, which is the most power-hungry thing a countertop appliance does. A kettle is the same. Knowing the wattage before the day you need it is cheaper than finding out at the breaker panel.

The short answer

Add up the wattage of everything that shares the circuit, and plan to run one high-draw appliance at a time. If a listing gives two wattages, assume the higher one.

Read the number off the listing

The Emeril's spec panel states 1700 W at 120 V. The 6-quart Chefman's spec panel states 1700 W at 120 V. The 8-quart Chefman states 1000 W in one place and 1700 W in another, which is flagged as FLG-038 and is exactly why both are printed here rather than one being chosen.

What 1700 W means in amps

At 120 V a 1700 W appliance draws roughly 14 amps. A standard US kitchen small-appliance circuit is 20 amps, and it is usually shared with the other countertop outlets in the room. Check the panel before assuming you have the headroom.

What else is on that circuit

The kettle, the coffee machine, the toaster and the microwave often share one circuit. Two 1700 W appliances running together, plus anything else, is how a holiday dinner ends in the dark. Run one high-draw appliance at a time.

Extension cords and power strips

A high-draw appliance belongs in a wall outlet. If the cord does not reach, the appliance belongs somewhere else, not on a strip. Follow the manufacturer's instructions, which are linked on the listing.

Why this is in a buying guide

Because the wattage is the one specification that can make an otherwise ideal appliance unusable in your kitchen. It is worth thirty seconds of arithmetic before you order.
