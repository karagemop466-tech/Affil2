How to read an air fryer listing: what “Hi-Fry” and 24 presets actually tell you

Every product page is three documents wearing one coat: a title written for search, bullets written to sell, and a structured table written to be filtered. They are maintained separately, and they disagree. Knowing which block a number came from is most of the skill, and it takes about a minute per appliance.

The short answer

Trust the structured field over the bullet and the title. Where two fields disagree, the number is unsettled — ask the seller instead of choosing the flattering one. And measure depth before you compare quarts: the counter decides the purchase, and a single layer decides the cooking.

01Three blocks, three jobs

The title is a keyword string: capacity, a couple of feature names, a colour. It is the least reliable place for a number and the most quoted one. The bullets (“About this item”) are marketing prose with figures dropped into them. The Product information block — Features & Specs, Measurements, Item details — is structured fields, and it is the closest thing to a data source on the page. Not a guarantee, though: it is filled in by the same seller, and on the pages in our register it sometimes contradicts the bullets and the title. When you cite a spec at anyone, including yourself, say which block it came from.

02When the same number appears twice and disagrees

These are all live on 2026-11-20 and all recorded in our register. The 8-quart Chefman prints 1700 watts in the Features & Specs fields and 1000 watts in a bullet; its title promises a 450 °F Hi-Fry option while the temperature fields say 400 °F maximum. The Emeril's title says 24 one-touch preset options and one bullet says 25; the title and the Included Components field list six accessories while a description field says seven. The dual-basket Chefman says 6 quarts in the title and bullets, and its Measurements table prints 6 litres, which is about 6.3 US quarts. None of these resolve into a single true number from the page alone. What a careful reader does with a disagreement like that: treat the figure as unsettled, ask the seller, and never quietly average the two.

03Features versus decoration

A word in a title is not a specification. “Sync Finish” on the dual-basket page describes a behaviour you can check on the spot: two baskets timed to end together. “Hi-Fry” is a mode with its own temperature ceiling — and it is exactly where the 450 versus 400 argument lives on the 8-quart. “4-in-1” counts cooking modes the seller decided to name, which tells you nothing about how well any of them works. “24 one-touch presets” counts buttons; the 8-quart here has four — French fries, chicken, meat, fish — and four honest buttons beat 24 you will never use. Certification wording (“cETL approved” on two of these pages, “NIST and NSF” on a thermometer page) is a claim on a listing; we have not seen the certificates and neither of us has.

04The numbers that actually decide the purchase

Depth first, capacity second, and both from the structured fields. From our register: 13.38 in deep for the dual-basket, 12.75 in for the 8-quart, 16.9 in for the French-door oven, which also needs room in front for two doors to swing. Then capacity, understood properly: quarts describe the container, and an air fryer cooks what hot air can reach in a single layer. Two independent 3-quart baskets do a different job from one 6-quart drawer, and both are a different job again from a 26-quart cavity whose own listing talks about a 12-inch pizza and a 6-pound chicken. Weight is what decides whether the appliance lives on the counter: 13.3 pounds and 12.8 pounds for the two Chefmans, and for the Emeril the listing simply does not state an item weight, so ours stays blank.

05What a preset count is, and what it is not

A preset is a stored pairing of a temperature and a run time. It adds no heat, no capacity and no method; it saves two button presses on a dish you repeat. That is the whole of it, which is why a count is a weak thing to compare: the 8-quart Chefman names four presets in its structured field (French fries, chicken, meat, fish) while the Emeril prints 24 in its title and description and 25 in the About-this-item bullets, on the same page, on the same day we read it. If you want the shortcuts that matter, look for the controls that fix a mistake rather than the ones that name a food: a +2-minute button, stated on the dual-basket listing, and an LED shake reminder, stated on the 8-quart. A frozen-chicken mode answers a question your dinner rarely asks.

06Four checks that cost one minute

(1) Find the number in the structured table before you accept it from a bullet. (2) If two fields disagree, ask the seller before ordering, not after. (3) Read what Included Components actually lists, because accessory counts and preset counts are where pages drift. (4) Write down the date you read the page. These listings change without notice, which is why every table on this site carries its date and its link rather than our memory.
