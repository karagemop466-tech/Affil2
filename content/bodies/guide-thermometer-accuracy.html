A thermometer that reads in one second is only useful if the number is right. Here is how to check yours, how often, and what to do when it drifts.

Why probes drift

A probe sensor sits inside a metal tube that is repeatedly heated, chilled and washed. Over time the electronics can shift slightly. That is normal and it is why every serious kitchen checks its probes on a schedule instead of trusting them indefinitely.

The ice-point method, step by step

  1. Fill a glass to the brim with crushed ice, then add cold water until it just covers the ice.
  2. Stir for a few seconds so the temperature is even throughout.
  3. Insert the probe at least an inch into the middle of the slush, without touching the glass wall or the bottom.
  4. Hold still and wait for the reading to settle.
  5. Record the number. A probe that settles near 32 F (0 C) is healthy.

Read the manual before you adjust anything

Many probes have a documented calibration offset procedure. Use theirs. Inventing an offset without recording it is how people end up with a thermometer that is confidently wrong.

The boiling-point method, and why the ice method is usually enough

Boiling water reads 212 F (100 C) at sea level, and lower as elevation rises. That makes boiling a worse reference unless you correct for altitude. The ice point is self-correcting at any elevation, which is why it is the standard check.

What to do when a probe fails the check

How often to check

Twice a year is a reasonable default for a home kitchen, plus once whenever a probe has been dropped or washed in a way the manual discourages. Put the date of the last check in a place you will see it, because the check nobody remembers to do is the check that does not happen.

Where the target numbers come from

They come from the USDA, and we do not restate them from memory. The FSIS safe minimum internal temperature chart sets poultry at 165 F, ground meats at 160 F, whole-muscle cuts at 145 F with a three-minute rest, fish at 145 F, and leftovers at 165 F.
