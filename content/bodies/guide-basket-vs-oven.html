The appliance aisle splits into two shapes: a drawer you pull, or a door you open. They solve different problems, and the wrong pick is the one you stop using in month three.

Both shapes move hot air around food to crisp the surface with far less oil than a deep fryer. What changes is how you load them, how you clean them, and how much of the counter they own. Those three things decide which one you actually keep using.

Start with what you cook most, not what you cook best

Write down the last ten things you cooked at home. Then answer one question: were any of them two components that needed different heat at the same time?

If the answer is usually yes, the shape of the decision changes. A basket air fryer gives you one chamber, one temperature and one timer. An oven-style unit gives you a flat rack you can load in one pass, and the French-door versions let you open half a door instead of swinging a hot drawer forward toward your wrists.

On capacity, ignore the number on the box at first

Quart capacity measures the chamber, not the food. A deep 6-quart basket and a flat 6-quart oven cavity hold the same volume but behave nothing alike, because crisping depends on air reaching surfaces. Read our capacity checklist before you compare two machines on this number alone.

Where the two-basket design earns its keep

A dual-basket machine is a third shape: two separate chambers with separate temperatures and timers, plus a sync-finish function that aims to land both at the same moment. That is a real advantage if you cook a protein and a vegetable together. It is dead weight if you mostly cook one thing, because you now have two baskets to wash and a wider footprint.

The question to ask before you buy either

Can I name the last three meals this would have made easier?

If those three meals come to mind immediately, the shape of the answer probably points at one style. If you have to invent them, no amount of preset count will fix that.
